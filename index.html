AgriTech research project · 2026

Listening for pests inside stored grain.

KOLOS is a low-cost acoustic probe that sits in a grain pile and listens for the faint sounds of insects, so farmers can catch an infestation long before the grain starts to heat up.

Designed, built and coded by Andrey Makarovsky · Letovo School, Moscow · for warehouses in my hometown of Orenburg

Why I built this

A problem you can't see until it's too late

I'm from Orenburg, one of Russia's biggest grain regions. The harvest there is a big part of the local economy, and the work does not end when the combines leave the fields. Grain can sit in a warehouse for months, and insects like the grain weevil can quietly multiply inside the pile.

In summer 2026 I visited a grain warehouse of about 3,000 tonnes in the Orenburg region and talked with its manager. They check grain the way most farms still do: someone pushes a hand probe into a few spots in the pile and looks at the samples. A few kilograms of grain have to stand in for hundreds of tonnes. Often the first clear sign of trouble is that the grain starts to heat up, and by then the infestation is already large.

Modern grain-monitoring systems exist, but most are imported. As trade routes narrowed, they became harder and more expensive to get, so smaller farms are left with manual sampling. I wanted to build something a small farm could actually afford and install by hand.

Insects chew, crawl and move grain kernels. Every one of those actions makes a tiny sound. What if the grain pile could tell us it was infested?

How it works

Listen. Analyse. Alert.

Instead of taking a sample once in a while, KOLOS listens all the time and raises a flag only when the activity looks like living insects, not random noise.

1

Listen

A perforated chamber at the tip of the probe sits inside the grain. A sensitive microphone picks up sounds from the grain around it.

2

Analyse

An ESP32 microcontroller measures the signal several times a second and compares it with the background level it learned during calibration.

3

Alert

Single bumps are ignored. Repeated activity over time moves the status from Normal to Attention to Alert, showing where to take a sample.

The prototype

From a CAD sketch to a working probe in one summer

I built the working prototype in July and August 2026 at home. I modelled every printed part in Autodesk Fusion 360, prepared them in OrcaSlicer and printed them on my Creality K1C.

Machine learning

Teaching the probe to tell insects from noise

A simple threshold can't tell a beetle from a truck driving past. So I recorded my own data with the prototype and trained a Random Forest classifier in Python (scikit-learn) to recognise four kinds of signal:

  • Silence: the quiet background
  • Single knocks: random one-off bumps
  • Repeated impulses: a metronome, my stand-in for insect activity
  • Airborne noise: sound coming through the air

Each 20-measurement window is turned into 12 features: level, spread, peaks and how regularly the signal repeats. The model trained on the first 70% of each recording and was tested on the last 30%, which it had never seen.

93.4%

accuracy on held-out data (267 of 286 windows). Every repeated-impulse window was recognised correctly.

What I learned

More than a gadget

Start with the people

Talking to a warehouse manager showed me what the people who store grain actually need: something simple to install by hand and a clear answer to where to take a sample, not raw data.

Measure before you claim

I learned to separate what I have measured from what I still expect. The prototype detects small impacts; proving it detects real insects in grain is the next experiment.

Hardware is iteration

The printed parts needed several design fixes before everything fit together. Each one taught me something about tolerances, assembly and how a real device is put together.
